Cash flow forecasting

7 min readLast checked 2026-09-28

A cash forecast follows bank timing, not accounting recognition. It shows whether the business can make each scheduled payment with the cash expected to arrive.

  • Invoices can count as revenue before the customer pays.
  • Inventory can use cash before it becomes an expense.
  • Debt principal uses cash but does not appear as an operating expense.

Each item separates earned profit from available cash. The forecast reconciles that timing week by week.

The 13-week cash flow

Illustrative assumptions, not observed results: amounts are in thousands of dollars. Receivables and new-sales cash are deposits expected that week. Parentheses are payments. Each ending balance becomes the next week's starting balance.

Week:                W1    W2    W3    W4
Starting cash:       500   670   940   540
+ Receivables:       250   300   200   180
+ New sales cash:    200   150   250   300
- Payroll:           (180) (0)   (180) (0)
- Rent:              (50)  (0)   (0)   (0)
- Vendors:           (20)  (120) (90)  (110)
- Taxes / other:     (30)  (60)  (580) (0)
Ending cash:         670   940   540   910

W1 is 500 + 250 + 200 - 180 - 50 - 20 - 30 = 670. W2 starts at 670 and ends at 940. W3 starts at 940 and ends at 540. This is a rolling cash example, not a recommended reserve or forecast horizon.

Inputs, receivables

  • Start from the reconciled bank balance, not the ledger's revenue total.
  • Place each invoice in the week the customer is expected to pay. Separate committed dates from estimates.
  • Enter payroll, rent, tax, debt, and vendor payments on their due dates.
  • Add scenarios for late collections, seasonality, contract loss, or a required purchase.

Choose the horizon from the obligations

Forecast far enough to see the next payrolls, debt dates, tax dates, large purchases, and seasonal low point. Refresh the near weeks from bank and invoice records. Keep later weeks clearly marked as estimates.

Where it breaks: A forecast can look precise while hiding disputed invoices, sales tax, debt principal, owner draws, or duplicate vendor payments. Reconcile the first week to the bank before trusting later weeks.

Do this today

  1. Reconcile today's bank balance.
  2. Place the next payroll, tax, debt, and vendor payments by date.
  3. Assign each receivable a committed or estimated collection week.
  4. Calculate the lowest ending balance and name the decision it triggers.
