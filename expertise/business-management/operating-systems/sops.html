SOPs that actually get used

4 min readLast checked 2026-09-28

SOPs. Standard Operating Procedures, are the documented "how we do this." They exist on paper at most businesses and in reality at few. The gap between the two is where quality, training time, and institutional knowledge all leak out.

Why SOPs matter

  • They make the expected outcome visible before work starts.
  • They give new staff a path that does not depend on memory.
  • They turn defects and exceptions into specific process changes.
  • Stored somewhere nobody looks
  • No one's accountable for keeping them current
  • Too long to read before just asking someone

The rules of useful SOPs

1. Written by the person who does the job

The operator drafts the real steps while doing the work. The owner checks policy, dependencies, and the final outcome.

3. Actionable, not theoretical

Use verbs, named systems, required fields, decision rules, and escalation paths. A new operator should know what evidence proves completion.

5. Versioned + dated

Record the owner, effective date, change reason, and superseded version. Review after the process changes, a defect repeats, or an incident exposes a gap.

The SOP template

TITLE: [What this SOP covers]
OWNER: [Who maintains this]
LAST UPDATED: [Date]
NEXT REVIEW: [Date]

WHEN TO USE THIS SOP
[One-paragraph description of the situation this covers]

OUTCOME
[What "done" looks like]

STEPS
1. [Specific action]
2. [Specific action]
   - Sub-step or nuance
3. [Specific action]
...

COMMON ISSUES + FIXES
- Problem X → do Y
- Problem Z → escalate to [person]

The adoption loop

  1. Run the SOP during real work.
  2. Mark the step where the operator paused, guessed, or used a workaround.
  3. Update the instruction and record the reason.
  4. Have another operator complete the revised version.
  5. Same questions get asked repeatedly
  6. SOPs are drastically different from actual practice
  7. Nobody knows who owns updating them

Use review triggers, not a universal calendar

Give each procedure an owner and explicit triggers: tool change, policy change, defect, incident, audit finding, or repeated exception. The change log should name what changed, why, and which version became effective.

Where it breaks: A procedure can be perfectly current and still fail when permissions, screenshots, templates, or escalation contacts are missing.

Do this today

  1. Choose one repeated task that produced a recent question.
  2. Run its SOP and mark the first unclear step.
  3. Update that step, owner, trigger, and version date.
  4. Ask a second person to complete the revised procedure.
