Compensation design

7 min readLast checked 2026-09-28

Compensation is the most visible expression of a company's priorities. Done well, it aligns incentives, attracts the right people, and makes retention cheap. Done casually, it generates resentment, turnover, and the wrong behaviors. Almost every early company I've seen starts with ad-hoc compensation decisions and spends years cleaning up the inequities those decisions created.

The components

  • Define levels by scope, judgment, and impact before attaching pay bands.
  • Set a documented range for each role and location using comparable market data.
  • Explain which outcomes drive variable pay and when they are measured.
  • Review offers and promotions for unexplained differences across comparable employees.
  • Every offer maps to a level + band; no "special case" compensation
  • Annual pay equity audit with specific actions taken on any findings
  • Variable comp is tight: rewards the right outcomes, not activity proxies
  • Retention offers never exceed what a proactive raise would've cost

Related: Role scorecard · Performance reviews · Hiring

Use a dated market and a visible level

Choose compensation data for the role, location, company type, and date. Define each level by scope, decisions, skills, and expected outcomes before attaching a range.

Use a dated compensation dataset filtered by role, level, location, and company type. Publish the leveling rubric, range method, variable-pay logic, and approval record.

For variable pay, connect each measure to a behavior the role controls. Show target, formula, cap, timing, reversals, and who approves exceptions.

Where it breaks: Mixed locations, stale titles, small samples, and discretionary exceptions can make a range look more precise than it is.

Do this today

  1. Write the level and scope for one role.
  2. Attach a dated, location-specific dataset.
  3. Map each incumbent to the same level method.
  4. Document the owner of exceptions and review.
