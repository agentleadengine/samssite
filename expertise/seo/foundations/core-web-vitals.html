Core Web Vitals

8 min readLast checked 2026-09-28

Core Web Vitals measure loading, interaction, and visual stability for real users. They can contribute to Search success, but a good score does not guarantee a top ranking. This page covers the three metrics, how to measure them, and how to fix them.

Why Google cares about speed

Google's whole business depends on people trusting search. If the top result is a page that takes six seconds to load and jumps around while loading, people bounce back to Google, click the next result, and eventually switch to a different search engine. Google loses. So Google penalizes slow, janky pages to protect its own product.

Core Web Vitals are Google's attempt to measure the specific flavors of slow and janky that drive users away. They're not abstract metrics. Each one tracks a specific thing that would annoy you if it happened on a page you were trying to use.

The three metrics, in plain English

LCP. Largest Contentful Paint

How long until the biggest visible element on the page has finished rendering. Usually that's a hero image, a big headline, or the main content block. It's Google's proxy for "how long until the page looks done."

Good is under 2.5 seconds. Bad is over 4. The targets are real, not aspirational. If your LCP is 5 seconds, Google's telling you something.

INP. Interaction to Next Paint

When a user clicks, taps, or types, how long until the page visibly responds. Replaces an older metric called FID in 2024. INP is harsher because it looks at the worst interaction during the whole session, not just the first.

Good is under 200ms. Anything over 500ms feels broken to users. INP is where over-engineered single-page apps and heavy JavaScript frameworks get caught.

CLS. Cumulative Layout Shift

How much the page moves around while loading. You know when you're about to click a button and then an ad loads and pushes everything down and you click the wrong thing? That's CLS, and Google measures it.

Good is under 0.1. Bad is over 0.25. The fix is usually just telling the browser ahead of time how much space to reserve for images, ads, and dynamic content.

How much CWV actually matters for ranking

Google says there is no single page-experience signal. Core Web Vitals can contribute to Search success, but good scores do not guarantee top rankings.

Field data vs lab data, the distinction that matters

There are two completely different ways to measure CWV, and confusing them is the number-one reason people mis-fix their sites.

  • Field data. Real user measurements collected from Chrome. This is what Google uses for ranking. It's what's reported in Search Console and in the Chrome User Experience Report (CrUX). It reflects actual user conditions, slow networks, old phones, real-world.
  • Lab data. Simulated measurements from a tool running in a controlled environment. Lighthouse and the "lab" section of PageSpeed Insights give you this. Useful for iterating on a fix quickly, but doesn't represent real users.

If your field data looks fine and your lab data looks bad, your users are fine. If your lab data looks fine and your field data looks bad, your users are suffering and you're not replicating their conditions in testing.

Where to actually measure

  • Google Search Console. Under "Core Web Vitals," you'll see field data on every URL on your site, bucketed Good/Needs Improvement/Poor. Start here. This is what Google sees.
  • PageSpeed Insights. Plug in a URL, get both field data and lab data for that page specifically. Good for drilling into one page.
  • Chrome DevTools. Open any page in Chrome, hit DevTools, go to the Performance or Lighthouse tab, run an audit. Great for development iteration.
  • CrUX API / BigQuery. For advanced teams, real-user data at scale across your whole site.

The fixes that actually move the numbers

The 80/20 of Core Web Vitals work

Don't try to chase perfect scores everywhere. The returns are wildly diminishing past "Good."

  1. Open Search Console. Find the pages in the "Poor" bucket. Those are the only ones where CWV is likely actively hurting you.
  2. Look at which metric is failing. LCP, INP, or CLS. Each has a specific fix family.
  3. Apply the obvious fix. Compressed images, deferred scripts, explicit image dimensions. 80% of CWV fixes are one of those three.
  4. Wait 28 days. CrUX data updates on a rolling 28-day window. You won't see field data reflect your fix for weeks.
  5. Repeat for the next page bucket. Don't try to fix everything at once.

Common traps that waste time

  • Chasing a perfect Lighthouse score. A 100 lab score doesn't guarantee a 100 field score. Users matter, lab numbers don't.
  • Over-engineering for CWV on a page nobody visits. If it doesn't rank and doesn't convert, its CWV doesn't matter. Fix the meaningful pages first.
  • Letting marketing's third-party scripts pile up unchecked. Every chat widget, analytics tool, and A/B testing script adds INP risk. Audit quarterly.
  • Fixing CLS by disabling animations users like. CLS punishes unexpected shifts, not all motion. Good layout animations don't count if they're on purpose.

What to do with this

Open Search Console now. Check the Core Web Vitals report. If everything's green, move on. If you have URLs in the Poor bucket, note which metric is failing and plan a fix sprint. Don't make it a side project, block a real week for it. Speed is one of the few SEO investments that pays off both in rank and in conversion at the same time.

Do this today

  1. Open the Core Web Vitals report and choose one affected group.
  2. Name the failing metric and one proposed change.
  3. Compare real-user data and conversions after deployment.

Limitation: A lab improvement may not match the experience of real users on different devices and networks.
