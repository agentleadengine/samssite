Title tags

8 min readLast checked 2026-09-28

A title element helps describe a page, but Google can generate the displayed title link from several sources. Write a unique, descriptive title for the reader, then measure the page's own clicks. This page covers the sources Google may use and a practical review process.

Google can generate title links from the title element, visible heading, prominent page text, anchor text, and structured data. It does not publish a fixed character or pixel limit.

Changing28 Sep 2026Source 01

Why title tags matter more than people realize

Two reasons. First, Google weights the title tag heavily in relevance scoring. It's the most prominent piece of text on your page from a search-engine perspective. Second, the title is the pitch. A user sees 10 results, scans the titles in under a second, and clicks one. Your title has about half a second of attention to sell the click.

Most SEOs optimize only for the first job, relevance, and write titles that match the keyword but read like tax forms. Their CTR is terrible. Great SEOs optimize for both. A good title matches what the searcher typed and makes the listing impossible not to click.

The two jobs of a title tag

The rules that actually matter

  • Put the primary keyword near the front. "Best CRM for Insurance Agents (2026 Guide)" beats "A Detailed 2026 Review: Best CRM Software You Can Use If You Are An Insurance Agent." The front-loaded keyword is a stronger relevance signal and readers scan left to right.
  • Keep the title concise and descriptive. Check how it appears for the page's current queries. Do not target a fixed pixel cutoff.
  • One unique title per page. Duplicates confuse Google and make a site look lazy. Even close variations should be distinct.
  • Make a specific promise. "SEO tips" is vague. "11 SEO tactics that doubled my traffic" is specific. Specific outperforms vague every time.
  • Match the intent of the query. If the query is informational, the title should promise information. If transactional, it should promise the action.

Patterns that work

Modifiers that boost CTR

Certain words act like magnets in search results. Add them when they fit naturally, not when they don't.

  • Numbers. "7 Ways" outperforms "Several Ways." Odd numbers tend to perform better than even ones (weird but repeatedly confirmed).
  • Year markers. "(2026)" or "Updated 2026" signal freshness, especially on queries where freshness matters.
  • "Best," "Complete," "Ultimate," "Honest." Qualifiers that promise depth or candor.
  • "Free" or "No Login." When true. High CTR on qualifying searches.
  • Brackets and parentheses. "[Updated]" or "(Step-by-Step)" visually stand out in the blue link.

Don't stack them. Two modifiers is plenty. Four and the title looks desperate.

Brand placement

Most sites put the brand at the end, separated by a pipe or a dash. "Best CRM for Insurance Agents | Acme CRM." Clean, scannable, and the brand gets a tiny authority boost without stealing pitch space.

Exceptions: the homepage, where brand-first is normal ("Acme CRM. Built for Insurance Agents"). Also pages that rank for branded queries, where putting the brand earlier helps.

What not to do

Title vs H1, use them differently

A common mistake is making your page title identical to your H1. They serve different audiences. Title tag is what Google shows and what a searcher sees before clicking. H1 is what the reader sees after clicking.

Title tag should be concise, keyword-front, pitch-forward. H1 can be longer, more conversational, more oriented to the reader who's already arrived. Write the title for search. Write the H1 for the reader.

When Google rewrites your title

Sometimes you'll notice Google showing a different title in the SERP than the one you wrote. It's allowed to do this. It does it when your title doesn't match the query well, when it's too long, when it looks stuffed, or when it has no brand but the query was branded.

You can't prevent rewrites, but you can reduce them. Check Search Console for your best pages. If Google is rewriting a title, that title is probably weak. Rewrite it yourself in a way that anticipates what Google would do.

The testing loop

Title tags are easy to iterate on because they cost nothing to change. The flow:

  1. Look at your top 20 pages in Search Console
  2. For each, note CTR and average position
  3. Find pages ranking in positions 3 to 10 with below-average CTR for that position
  4. Rewrite the title with a sharper promise or better modifier
  5. Wait 2 weeks and check CTR
  6. Keep what works, revert what doesn't

Record query, position, impressions, and CTR before a title change. Compare the same measures after enough comparable impressions accrue.

What to do with this

Today, right now, open Search Console. Look at your top 10 pages. For each, ask the one question that matters: if I saw my title on a results page, would I click it over the competition? If not, rewrite it this afternoon.

Next: meta descriptions, the second line of your SERP listing and the one most sites get wrong.

Do this today

  1. Choose one page with stable query data.
  2. Write a concise title that matches visible content.
  3. Compare CTR by query and position after the change.

Limitation: Google can rewrite title links, so a title edit does not guarantee the displayed wording.
