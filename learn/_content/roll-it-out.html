A good office rollout is small, visible, and boring in the best way. Give one person clear ownership, keep a shared list of prompts, check in for fifteen minutes each week, and write down what may and may not go into the tool.

Name one owner

The owner is not the person who must answer every AI question. They are the person who keeps the basic rules clear. They choose the approved tool, maintain the prompt list, collect problems, and make sure a new hire knows the boundary before using it with real work.

Without an owner, a rollout becomes a collection of private experiments. One person pastes in sensitive information, another buys a subscription, and nobody knows which customer reply was drafted by what. A single owner gives the team one place to ask, "Is this okay to use?"

For a small team, the best AI owner is usually the person who already improves office processes and can say no when a task is not ready. It does not need to be the most technical person.

My takeFor small office rollouts

Make one shared prompt list

Put the useful prompts in a shared document. Each entry needs a title, when to use it, the exact prompt, an example of the input, and who owns the facts inside it. Keep only prompts that someone has actually used and reviewed. Delete duplicates and old versions so a new teammate does not pick the wrong one.

Start with three prompts: a customer reply draft, a thread summary, and a rough-notes-to-SOP prompt. Those are common enough to teach the habit without putting anyone in charge of an automated decision.

Hold a fifteen-minute weekly check

Once a week, ask three questions. What did someone use again? Where did a draft go wrong? What prompt or rule should change? Keep the conversation concrete. "It was bad" is hard to fix. "It invented a Friday deadline" tells you to add a source rule and a deadline check.

The owner should write down one decision from every check. It may be small: add a warning to the FAQ prompt, stop using AI for a certain customer task, or move a good prompt to the shared list. Those small decisions become your real policy.

Usage is a better signal than enthusiasm. If people choose the same safe task again without being reminded, you have found something worth standardizing.

My takeAfter a short trial

Start with a short usage policy

Your policy does not need to sound like a legal contract. It needs to help a busy person make the right call. Keep it next to the shared prompts, then ask your lawyer or security lead to review it if the work is sensitive or regulated.

  • Use only the office-approved AI tool for company work.
  • Use AI for drafts, summaries, and organization. A person reviews anything shared outside the office.
  • Do not paste Social Security numbers, card numbers, health information, passwords, or login codes.
  • Do not let AI make final decisions about prices, people, coverage, legal matters, or money.
  • Report a mistake, strange output, or privacy concern to the tool owner.

OpenAI says Business, Enterprise, Edu, and API data is not used for training by default. Anthropic says Team and Enterprise data is not used to train by default unless an organization opts in. Exact account terms still need to be checked for the plan your office uses.

Expand only after the habit works

Do not roll out every feature at once. Start with two people and three safe tasks. Once the prompts, review step, and policy are being used, add another team member or a second type of work. This makes it easier to spot what changed when something goes wrong. Keep the first expansion equally small.
