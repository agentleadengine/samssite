The useful rule is simple: AI writes the first draft, a person owns the final answer. A two-minute check catches the mistakes that sound smooth, look complete, and still cause trouble once a customer or coworker relies on them.

Why a quick check matters

An assistant can make a mistake in a polished sentence. It can confuse two names, pull the wrong date from a long thread, or fill a missing detail with a plausible guess. The risk rises when the answer contains a promise, a price, a quote, or a statement someone might repeat outside the office.

Review is not busywork added after AI. It is the trade: you save time on the blank page, then spend attention on the details that need a human. For a short customer email, that often takes less time than writing the email from nothing.

Do not ask, "Does this sound good?" Ask, "What could be wrong if this went out exactly as written?" That question changes a quick skim into a real review.

Your two-minute checklist

Read this list in order when the answer will be shared, sent, published, or used to make a decision.

  1. Names: Are people, companies, products, and pronouns right?
  2. Numbers: Are prices, quantities, percentages, totals, and account references right?
  3. Dates: Does every deadline, appointment, and sequence match the source?
  4. Promises: Did it promise a refund, result, delivery, coverage, or response time you have not approved?
  5. Quotes: Is quoted language exact, and does it belong to the right person or source?
  6. Links: Do the links open, and do they support what the sentence says?
  7. Tone: Does it sound like your office, and is it appropriate for this person?
  8. Private data: Did you include or repeat anything that should not be in this message or tool?

Match the check to the consequence

Not every draft needs the same level of care. An internal brainstorm can get a light read. A customer response about a price needs the source open beside it. A medical, legal, employment, financial, or safety statement may need the person responsible for that decision to review it. AI can help organize information, but it does not take responsibility for the outcome.

For a spreadsheet, use a sample check. Compare ten rows against the original data. For a summary, compare its key points against the source thread. For a web answer, open the links before you repeat any claim. Do not trust a citation just because one appears under the text.

Web search tools can return current information with citations, including Claude's cited web-search responses. The sources still need to be opened and checked because a model can misunderstand or misstate what it found.

Build a review habit into the job

Put the check where the work happens. Add "AI draft reviewed" to the last step of a shared process. Ask the person who owns the customer relationship to read customer-facing drafts. Keep a final decision with the person who knows the policy, the numbers, or the client history.

When the same mistake repeats, do not only fix that one answer. Improve the saved prompt. Add "use only the approved price list" or "say not stated when a due date is missing." A better prompt and a short check reinforce each other.

When you find an error after something has already gone out, handle the customer or coworker problem first. Then save a short note about what happened, which check missed it, and what the process will change. This turns an uncomfortable mistake into a specific improvement instead of a reason to stop learning.

Keep the checklist close to the send button, shared inbox, or handoff queue. A checklist in a policy document nobody opens will not help at the busy moment when a clean-looking draft is most tempting to trust.

Do this today

  1. Print or copy the eight-point checklist into your shared prompt document.
  2. Use AI to draft one low-risk email or summary.
  3. Check it against the original source, line by line for names, dates, and promises.
  4. Add one rule to the prompt based on the mistake you caught, or the question you had to answer.
