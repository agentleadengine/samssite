A familiar voice, logo, or writing style is no longer enough to approve a payment. The safe habit is simple: pause, verify through a contact you already trust, and require a second person for payment changes.

Know the stop signals

A payment request needs extra checking when it brings urgency, secrecy, new bank details, an unfamiliar invoice, or a change in normal language. The message may look like it came from an owner, employee, or regular vendor. Treat the changed request as the risk, not the quality of the voice or image.

Do not reply with the phone number or link inside the request. Do not let the caller transfer you to a supposed supervisor. Open the saved vendor record or company directory and use the contact details already there.

The FTC received more than 1 million imposter scam reports for 2025. Reported losses totaled $3.5 billion, including about $1 billion attributed to business impersonators.

Those totals show the consequence, not the odds that one message is fake. They also do not mean AI was involved in every report. Your procedure should work whether the request came from a cloned voice, a stolen email account, or an ordinary liar.

Use the two-channel check

Put this short protocol beside the person who pays invoices:

  1. Stop when a request changes payment details, adds a new vendor, or claims an executive exception.
  2. Call the vendor or executive on a known number from your saved records.
  3. Read back the request, amount, account change, and effective date.
  4. Require a second approver before changing payment details or releasing the payment.

The second approver repeats the check. They should not approve because the first person says the voice sounded right. Save the confirmation with the invoice or vendor record.

IC3 advises businesses to verify account-change requests through a secondary channel or two-factor authentication. It also advises checking the full sender address and monitoring accounts for irregularities.

A payment rule should not depend on spotting a fake. Build it so a perfect imitation still fails the callback and second-approval steps.

My takeFor small-office payment controls

A $4,800 voice message

An office manager receives a voice message that sounds like the owner. The speaker asks for a $4,800 wire to a new vendor account before noon. The message says the owner is in a meeting and cannot answer questions.

The manager does not use the callback number in the message. She calls the owner's saved number. The owner says he made no request. She checks the vendor list and finds no approved change. The second approver records the failed check and blocks the payment.

The useful question was not, “Does this sound fake?” The useful question was, “Did the known person confirm this changed payment request through our saved channel?” That question has an answer the office can record.

Keep the evidence and act quickly

Keep the original email, voice message, invoice, sender address, phone number, and payment instructions. Do not edit the files or forward them so many times that the original becomes hard to find.

If money was sent, contact the financial institution immediately and request a recall. Report suspected fraud through the official FTC and IC3 sites. Follow the company's incident process and tell the people responsible for the affected account.

Where this breaks

No visual, audio, or AI detector replaces independent verification. A detector can be wrong. A real person can also sound unusual when rushed, sick, or on a poor connection.

Reported complaint totals understate incidents that were never reported. They also cover different kinds of fraud. Use the figures to understand scale, not to claim that every fake invoice uses AI.

This is a payment verification routine, not legal advice. Ask your lawyer about notice, evidence, and reporting duties that apply to your business.

Do this today

  1. Choose the saved phone number used to verify each regular vendor.
  2. Require a callback for every new vendor or change in payment details.
  3. Name the second approver for executive requests and payment changes.
  4. Run the $4,800 scenario with the two people who pay invoices.
