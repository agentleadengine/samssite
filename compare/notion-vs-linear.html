Notion vs Linear

A flexible workspace vs an opinionated issue tracker. Verified 2026 pricing and where each one earns its seat.

Notion and Linear get compared constantly, but they are not close substitutes. Notion is a flexible workspace where you assemble docs, wikis, and databases into whatever system you want. Linear is an opinionated, fast issue tracker for software teams that would rather adopt a proven workflow than design their own. The real decision is which tool becomes the source of truth for engineering work, and whether you fund the other one next to it.

Pricing

Notion's paid plans run $10 per member per month for Plus and $20 for Business on annual billing, with monthly billing roughly 20 percent higher. The Business tier matters more than it used to because Notion now bundles its AI features there: Notion Agent, AI Meeting Notes, and enterprise search across connected apps like Slack and GitHub. If your team wants Notion AI, price the Business tier, not Plus.

Linear's free plan is a real trial: unlimited members, two teams, and a 250 issue cap. Basic is $10 per user per month billed annually and removes the issue cap. Business is $16 and adds unlimited teams, private teams, guest accounts, and the Zendesk and Intercom integrations. Enterprise adds SAML and SCIM on custom pricing.

Here is the number nobody puts on a pricing page: running both tools at their business tiers is $36 per user per month. That is what plenty of software companies actually pay, so decide deliberately which tool gets the expensive tier and who truly needs a seat in each.

When to pick Notion

Pick Notion when the job is knowledge: a company wiki, specs, meeting notes, onboarding docs, and lightweight databases for things like content calendars, hiring pipelines, and vendor lists. Non-engineering teams settle into Notion comfortably because everything is a page and nothing forces a workflow on them.

Its flexibility is both the draw and the tax. You can model almost any process with databases, relations, and views, which helps when your workflow does not match any off-the-shelf tool. But somebody has to build and maintain that system, and in my experience it quietly becomes a part-time job for whoever cares most about it.

I would not run a serious engineering team's issue tracker in Notion. It can be done with databases and templates, but it needs constant gardening, and large shared databases are exactly where Notion's performance complaints tend to show up.

When to pick Linear

Pick Linear when a software team needs to plan and ship. Issues, cycles, projects, and triage come pre-designed, the app stays fast, and most actions have a keyboard shortcut. The opinionated defaults are the product: you adopt a working process instead of spending meetings inventing one.

The free plan makes this cheap to test. Unlimited members with a 250 issue cap is enough to run a real team for a few weeks and see whether the workflow sticks. If it does, Basic at $10 removes the cap, and Business at $16 adds the support-tool integrations that connect customer tickets to engineering work.

What Linear is not is a wiki. Project documents cover specs attached to active work, but company knowledge, processes, and anything a non-engineer writes still needs a home somewhere else.

What most comparisons miss

The overlap between these tools is narrower than the comparison posts suggest. They compete on project tracking and almost nothing else. The practical questions are where issues live, where docs live, and how the two link together, since pasting Linear issue links into Notion pages is how most teams end up stitching them.

There is also a migration asymmetry worth knowing before you commit. Moving issues out of a Notion database into Linear is a mostly painless export. Untangling a three-year-old company wiki is not. Your Notion structure decisions compound for years, while the issue tracker is comparatively easy to swap. That argues for putting more care into how you set up Notion than into the tracker debate everyone focuses on.

My verdict

I treat this as two decisions, not one. Linear for engineering work: issues, cycles, and project planning, because the speed and the ready-made workflow pay off daily. Notion for everything written: wiki, specs, meeting notes, and cross-team databases. If budget forces a single choice for a small software team, I would keep Linear on Basic and use Notion's free plan or plain files for docs. What I would not do is run sprints for engineers inside Notion. Both tools are cheap relative to the hours they touch.
