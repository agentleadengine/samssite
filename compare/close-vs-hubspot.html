Close vs HubSpot

A phone-first sales CRM vs the platform that wants your whole go-to-market. Compared on verified 2026 pricing.

Close is a CRM built around outreach: calling, SMS, and email live in every plan, and the product assumes your revenue comes from reps working a pipeline. HubSpot Sales Hub is one wing of a much larger platform that also spans marketing, service, and a large app marketplace. Entry prices look similar, but costs grow differently: Close scales by seats, HubSpot by tier jumps, onboarding fees, and add-ons. Decide based on whether a phone-heavy sales team or a marketing-fed funnel is the engine of your business.

Pricing

Close prices per user per month, with a meaningful annual discount. Solo is $9 billed annually or $19 monthly and is capped at one user. Essentials is $35 annually ($49 monthly), Growth is $99 ($109), and Scale is $139 ($149). Every plan includes calling, SMS, and email with usage-based telecom costs on top, plus the Chloe AI agent with a monthly credit allowance. Workflows and the power dialer arrive at Growth; the predictive dialer, roles and permissions, and call coaching at Scale. Add-ons include premium phone numbers at $19 per line and a call assistant at $50 per month plus $0.02 per minute. There is no onboarding fee, and there is a 14-day free trial.

HubSpot Sales Hub starts free for up to two users. Starter was listed at $7 per seat per month billed annually ($20 month to month) when I checked. Professional is $90 per seat annually ($100 monthly) plus a one-time $1,500 onboarding fee, and Enterprise starts at $150 per seat with a $3,500 onboarding fee. Each tier includes a monthly allowance of HubSpot Credits (500, 3,000, and 5,000 respectively) that power the AI features, with extra credits at about a penny each on annual terms. I checked both vendors' pricing pages in August 2026.

When to pick Close

Pick Close when your team lives on the phone. The dialers are the product: a power dialer at Growth, a predictive dialer at Scale, call recording, and coaching tools, all in the same place reps log activity. Nothing needs to be bolted on, and in my own cold-calling work the difference between a CRM with a native dialer and one without shows up in daily call volume, not in a feature list.

It is also the simpler purchase. Per-seat pricing, no onboarding fee, and a two-week trial mean a small team can be running sequences within days. If you have no marketing function to serve, you are not paying for one.

The honest caveat: Close is sales software only. If you later need landing pages, email marketing at scale, or a service desk, you will be integrating other tools.

When to pick HubSpot

Pick HubSpot when sales is one part of a larger motion. The free CRM is a real on-ramp for a company with no system at all, and the Starter tier is cheap enough to put every customer-facing person in one database. The value shows up when marketing attribution, lifecycle stages, and service tickets share records with the sales pipeline, which is something Close does not attempt.

The ecosystem is the other argument. The app marketplace, the supply of agencies and admins who know the platform, and the depth of documentation mean you will rarely be stuck. For a company that expects to grow into Professional or Enterprise across multiple hubs, standardizing early on HubSpot avoids a painful migration later.

What most comparisons miss

Sticker prices at the entry level are close to meaningless here. HubSpot's $7 Starter seat is an on-ramp price, and the step to Professional is $90 per seat plus $1,500 in onboarding, a jump most growing teams eventually face. Close's $9 Solo plan is capped at one user, so a real team pays $35 to $139 per user. Model the tier you will actually need in a year, not the one on the banner.

Watch the usage meters too. Close bills calling and SMS by usage on top of the subscription, and both vendors now meter AI through credit allowances. Heavy AI use is a variable cost line that did not exist on CRM bills two years ago.

And consider gravity. HubSpot's free tier makes it easy to enter, and its all-in-one design makes it hard to leave. Close is easier to replace, which cuts both ways: less lock-in, but also less reason for the rest of the company to standardize on it.

My verdict

I run cold-call-driven outreach myself, and for a sales-led team of two to twenty reps I would pick Close: the dialers, the sequences, and the lack of an onboarding fee fit how that kind of team actually works. I would pick HubSpot for a company where marketing generates the pipeline, where free matters at the start, or where sales, marketing, and service need one database. The wrong answer is buying HubSpot for a phone team and then paying to bolt on the calling stack Close ships by default.
