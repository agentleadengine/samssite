Figma vs Canva

A precision tool for building products vs a template engine for producing content.

These tools get compared constantly and they are barely competitors. The real decision axis is what you are making. Figma is a precision tool for designing software: interfaces, design systems, and prototypes that developers build from. Canva is a template engine for producing finished content fast: social posts, decks, flyers, and videos made by people with no design training. The question is not which is better, it is which job you have.

The seat types matter. In Figma, only people who design need full seats. Developers can take $12 Dev seats and stakeholders $3 Collab seats, so a team of two designers, four engineers, and a PM costs far less than seven full seats. Canva is simpler: everyone who edits pays the same rate.

When to pick Figma

Pick Figma if you are designing software. It is the industry standard for a reason: real components and variables, shared libraries that keep a design system consistent, multiplayer editing, and Dev Mode so engineers can inspect exactly what to build. If a developer will ever implement what you are drawing, Figma is the correct tool and Canva is not in the conversation.

It also wins for anything that needs precision at scale. Auto layout, constraints, and prototyping let you design one flow and have it hold up across screen sizes and states. The free Starter plan is generous enough for a solo founder to mock up an entire app without paying, and the seat model keeps costs reasonable because only actual designers need the $16 full seats.

The cost is the learning curve. Figma is a professional tool, and someone opening it to make a quick Instagram post will have a bad afternoon. That is not what it is for.

When to pick Canva

Pick Canva if the goal is finished content, today, made by whoever is available. A competent-looking social graphic, one-pager, or pitch deck takes minutes because you start from a designed template rather than a blank canvas. For a small business without a designer on staff, that is not a compromise, it is the entire value.

Canva has also quietly become a broad content suite: video editing, presentations, printing, scheduling posts to social platforms, and a fast-growing pile of AI features for generating and editing images. The Business tier at $25 per user adds brand kits and locked templates, which is the practical answer to the real small-team problem of everyone's flyers looking different. Note that Canva restructured its team offering, so older articles quoting roughly $10 per person for the legacy Teams plan no longer reflect what new customers pay.

The ceiling is real. Template-based work looks like template-based work, and pixel-level control is limited. But most marketing content does not need to clear that ceiling, it needs to ship on schedule.

What most comparisons miss

Most comparisons rank these tools on shared features and miss that the overlap is shrinking from both directions on purpose. Figma has pushed into Canva's territory with Figma Slides and marketing-oriented tools, and Canva has pushed toward professional workflows, including acquiring the Affinity suite of professional design apps. Yet neither has displaced the other, because the buyer is different: Figma is bought by product teams as infrastructure, Canva is bought by marketing teams as a content factory. Evaluating them on features hides the fact that they answer to different budgets and different definitions of done.

The practical insight for small companies is that this is usually a both decision, not an either decision. Product mockups in Figma, marketing output in Canva, roughly $16 plus $18 a month if one person wears both hats. Forcing marketing into Figma burns hours on work a template solves, and forcing product design into Canva produces mockups a developer cannot build from. The tools are cheap enough that using each for its actual job beats consolidating.

My verdict

This one has a clean answer because the tools solve different problems. If you design software, use Figma, full stop, and lean on its cheap Dev and Collab seats to bring the rest of the team in. If you produce marketing content without a designer, use Canva and do not feel like you settled, because shipping decent content fast is the job. Small teams that do both should run both. The combined cost is about what one streaming bundle runs, and each tool is much better than the other at its own job.
