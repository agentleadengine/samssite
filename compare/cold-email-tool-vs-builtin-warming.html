Dedicated warming vs Built-in warming

Standalone warming services against the warmup bundled into cold email platforms, and the cost and policy tradeoffs behind the choice.

The real question is whether warming deserves its own per-mailbox fee when every serious sending platform now bundles it for free. Dedicated services like MailReach and Warmy charge per inbox and add better monitoring. Platforms like Instantly and Smartlead include unlimited warmup in their base plans. And hanging over both options is the same uncomfortable fact: automated warming networks run against Google's policies, so the choice is really about cost and reporting, not about a safe option versus a risky one.

Cost drivers

Dedicated warming scales linearly with mailbox count. MailReach lists $19.50 per mailbox per month on monthly billing, with about 20% off annually, so a typical 30-inbox cold outreach setup costs roughly $585 a month for warming alone. Warmy no longer publishes flat plans and instead quotes volume-based pricing on the warmup traffic you want, which usually means talking to sales.

Built-in warming is flat. Smartlead's plans start at $39 a month and Instantly's Growth plan at $47 a month, and both include unlimited connected email accounts with unlimited warmup. Adding your tenth or thirtieth inbox costs nothing extra on the warming side. That difference in cost shape, linear versus flat, is the whole economic argument, and it grows with every inbox you add.

The spend that actually determines your results sits elsewhere anyway: the mailboxes and domains themselves, list quality and verification, and the discipline to ramp sending volume slowly. Warming is a small line item next to those whichever way you buy it.

When to pick Dedicated warming

I would pay for a dedicated service mainly for the monitoring, not the warming. MailReach and similar tools bundle inbox placement tests and deliverability dashboards that tell you whether mail lands in the inbox, promotions, or spam at specific providers. If you run deliverability for clients and need evidence to show them, that reporting is the product, and the warming traffic is almost incidental.

A dedicated tool is also independent of your sending stack. If you switch from one sending platform to another, or you run mailboxes across several tools, a standalone warming service keeps working while everything else changes. Agencies juggling many client accounts feel this more than solo senders do.

What I would not do is buy a dedicated service because the warming itself is supposedly better. That claim is hard to verify from the outside, both models rely on networks of inboxes exchanging and rescuing emails, and no vendor publishes controlled comparisons.

When to pick Built-in warming

For almost everyone sending their own cold email, built-in is the right call. It costs nothing extra, it needs no second account, and it covers every inbox you connect at any scale. The base plans of the major platforms, $39 at Smartlead and $47 at Instantly, already include it without per-mailbox charges.

Built-in warming is also operationally simpler in the way that matters day to day: the tool that warms the mailbox is the tool that sends from it, so warmup can wind down automatically as real volume ramps up, and you have one dashboard instead of two. Fewer moving parts means fewer connected apps touching your mailboxes, which is worth something on its own.

If you later find you need placement testing, you can buy that separately when the need is real. Starting with a per-mailbox warming subscription before you have evidence of a deliverability problem is paying to solve a problem you may never have.

What most comparisons miss

Neither option is the compliant one. Google's policies prohibit artificially inflating engagement, and Google moved against automated warmup tools in 2023, cutting off the access many of them used with Gmail accounts. Every warming network, dedicated or built-in, works by simulating opens, replies, and spam-folder rescues. I still think warming is standard practice in cold email, but anyone telling you one category is safe and the other is not is selling something.

The bigger miss is that warming is a minor input. Since February 2024, Google and Yahoo require bulk senders to authenticate with SPF, DKIM, and DMARC, offer one-click unsubscribe, and keep spam complaints below 0.3%. Those requirements, plus list verification and a slow volume ramp on aged domains, decide your deliverability. No warming tool, at any price, rescues a sender who fails them.

My verdict

Use the warming built into your sending platform. It is free at any inbox count, it is operationally simpler, and there is no solid evidence the paid networks warm better. Pay for a dedicated service only when you need what actually differentiates it, which is placement testing and client-grade reporting, and treat that as a monitoring purchase, not a warming purchase. Then put the money and attention you saved into authentication, list quality, and a patient ramp, because that is what deliverability actually runs on.
